Orion

Orion rose from the Aegean slowly, because a blind man does nothing quickly on black water. The sea reached his waist though the bottom lay a hundred fathoms down; that was Poseidon's blood in him, the old gift that let him walk where ships went. A boy rode on his shoulders, bare heels against Orion's ribs and one soot-black hand tangled in his hair. 'Gull to your left,' Cedalion said. Orion turned toward the wings, raised his bow, and held. He could hear each feather. He could not see the sky. 'East,' the boy reminded him. 'Sun first. Vengeance after.'

On Chios, King Oenopion had promised Orion his daughter Merope if the hunter cleared the island's fields of wild beasts. Orion filled the palace yard with tusks, antlers, hides, and proof. The king delayed the wedding, then delayed it again. Orion answered refusal with wine and went to Merope's chamber after she had barred the door. Oenopion's men seized him before dawn. The king burned out his eyes, dragged him to the shore, and left him among the nets. That night Orion learned what no hunt had taught him: a man could become the beast in a single doorway.

He followed the hammering of the Cyclopes to Lemnos, crawling whenever the ground vanished beneath his feet. Hephaestus found him at the forge entrance, enormous, bleeding, and still trying to threaten anyone who came near. The god of fire did not restore his sight. He set Cedalion, his smallest forge-boy, on Orion's shoulders and told him to walk toward sunrise. So the blind hunter crossed the sea with a child as his eyes. Cedalion named rocks, sails, storms, and once a dolphin that followed them for half a day. Orion began by giving orders. Before they reached the edge of dawn, he had learned to listen.

Helios met them where the morning first touched the water. Sunlight entered Orion's ruined eyes like molten gold. He screamed, fell to his knees without sinking, and opened them upon a world returned: red clouds, green sea, Cedalion's blackened grin. Orion's first clear sight was not Oenopion. It was the boy who had carried him by speaking. His second was Chios on the western horizon. He went back anyway. But Oenopion had hidden in a bronze chamber beneath the earth. Orion broke palace doors and split stone floors until Cedalion said, 'You have your eyes. That does not mean you can see him.' Orion lowered his club and left the island alive.

On Crete he found Artemis running beside a white hind. She knew his name, his crimes, and every boast he had ever made. She also knew he could bring down a charging boar at two hundred paces. The goddess allowed him to hunt beside her under one condition: kill what threatens the balance, never the balance itself. For a season they crossed the mountains as equals. Orion stopped filling courtyards with trophies. He learned the paths mothers used to lead their young to water. When villagers called him the greatest hunter alive, Artemis watched to see whether he would correct them. At first, he did.

Then came the feast at Knossos, the sweet wine, and a room full of men eager to hear a giant praise himself. Orion said there was no creature on earth he could not kill. He said, laughing, that given a year he could empty the whole world of beasts. Deep below the hall, Gaia heard him. The ground opened the next morning and released a scorpion the size of a chariot, black shell shining, tail curved over its back like a second moon. Orion's first arrow shattered. His second glanced away. The scorpion crossed a wheat field without bending a single stalk and came directly for him.

Orion ran toward the coast, not from fear but because Artemis's sacred groves lay inland. The scorpion followed over rock, sand, and the bodies of the hounds that tried to stop it. At the waterline, Orion stepped onto the sea. He waded farther though the waves never climbed above his chest. When the scorpion entered the surf, he dove beneath the foam, circled, and rose behind it with one arrow left. Water streamed from his hair and bowstring. The creature lifted its sting. Orion aimed not at its armor but at the pale joint beneath its tail, the one vulnerable place Artemis had taught him every living thing must have.

He loosed. The arrow struck true. So did the sting. Poison entered Orion's heel and climbed through him colder than the sea. Artemis reached the shore as the scorpion collapsed, its legs carving trenches in the sand. She held Orion upright while the water tried to carry his weight. 'I can ask Zeus to raise you beyond its reach,' she said. Orion remembered Merope behind the barred door, Oenopion beneath the earth, Cedalion patiently naming the world, and every beast he had killed to hear men cheer. 'Do not make me innocent,' he said. 'Make me useful. Let sailors find their road by me. Let hunters see what follows a boast.'

Zeus placed Orion among the winter stars with his belt, his sword, and his bow forever lifted. Gaia raised the scorpion too, but on the opposite side of the sky. When Scorpio climbs, Orion descends; when Orion rises, the scorpion is driven below the horizon. Cedalion grew old steering ships by the bright shoulders of the man he had once guided toward dawn. And every winter, above the same black water, Orion steps out of the eastern sea. He still looks like the greatest hunter in the world. Now, when the last creature turns away unharmed, he knows enough to lower the bow.
